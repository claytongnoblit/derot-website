# DeRot — A/B Test, Three Cells

*August 4, 2026. Winner drives website copy, so each cell is a different argument the homepage could lead with — a symptom, a contrast, or a stance.*

**Two constraints applied throughout:**

1. Every hook promises something the app delivers. No implied reduction in usage, no lockout, no usage report.
2. Nothing tells the reader who they are. No claims about their character, their identity, or who they stop being when they scroll.

Each cell opens on its strongest line, and the three run at deliberately different lengths.

**No mechanic narration.** None of these explain how DeRot works. Each one closes on an invitation to pick a single app the reader would rather feel calm using — phrased differently in each cell so the set doesn't read as one ad three times. That invitation is also literally the first thing that happens in onboarding, and the free tier is one app, so the ad promises exactly the experience that follows the install.

---

## Cell A — The demonstration

*Symptom-led · short · 47 words · control (closest to the current homepage)*

**Primary text** — count matches the in-app Calm technique exactly

> **Stop scrolling for a second.**
>
> Breathe in — 1, 2, 3, 4.
>
> Breathe out — 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6.
>
> How do you feel? More centered? Calmer?
>
> Now pick any app you'd like to pause you for a mindful breath, mid-scroll.

**Headline:** Your apps wind you up. DeRot brings you back down.

**Description:** Start with one app.

**Alt version — shorter count, lower ask on first exposure**

> **Stop scrolling for a second.**
>
> Breathe in — 1, 2, 3.
>
> Breathe out — 1, 2, 3, 4.
>
> How do you feel? More centered? Calmer?
>
> Now pick any app you'd like to pause you for a mindful breath, mid-scroll.

*Hook note: the ad is seen mid-scroll, so it performs the product instead of describing it. The count matters — "take a breath" is an instruction to interpret, whereas numbers are something to follow, and following them takes about as long as the breath does. The exhale is longer than the inhale, which is the actual mechanism the app is built on.*

*"Breathe in" / "breathe out" carry the instruction — bare numbers left the reader guessing at what was being asked. And "how do you feel?" asks rather than tells, which keeps the ad consistent with the rule the other two follow: the reader reports their own state, the ad never assigns one.*

*"Mindful" is kept deliberately. It's the one wellness-register word in the set and the brief's don't-say column leans against that vocabulary, so flagging it for Clayton is worthwhile — but it's mild, widely understood, and it earns its place here by naming what the reader just did.*

*Two production notes. Keep the counts on separate lines: run together in a paragraph they read in half a second and the reader never actually breathes. And keep this to feed placements only — the premise is false in search results or Apple Search Ads, where nobody is scrolling.*

*The 4/6 count is now primary because it's exactly what the reader meets inside the app — the ad and the first session teach the same rhythm. The 3/4 alt is a lower ask on a first exposure and worth rotating if 4/6 shows drop-off.*

## Cell B — Anti-blocker

*Contrast-led · medium · 63 words*

**Primary text**

> **Being locked out of your own phone felt worse than the scrolling did.**
>
> So you turned it off. And nothing changed.
>
> Try the other direction. Choose the app that winds you up the most — just the one — and let a slow breath land in the middle of it.
>
> Nothing gets locked. You come out calmer than you went in.

**Headline:** Locking yourself out didn't work. Try the opposite.

**Description:** Pick one app to start.

**Alt hooks to rotate**

- You lasted four days with the lockout on, didn't you.
- Locked yourself out of your phone yet? How did that go.
- Turns out you can't willpower your way out of a feed.

*Hook note: the previous version ("every other app in this category…") assumed the reader thinks in app categories and knows they're being sold software. This one leads on a lived experience instead — anyone who has tried a lockout recognizes it immediately, and anyone who hasn't still understands it. The category argument arrives later, once the ad has earned it.*

## Cell C — The reframe

*Reframe-led · long · 101 words*

**Primary text**

> **The problem isn't the scroll. It's how the scroll makes you feel.**
>
> Restless. Behind. Left out. Wound up over nothing in particular.
>
> Cutting it out cold turkey doesn't touch that, which is probably why it never lasted.
>
> So try it the other way round. Take the app that leaves you feeling worst — and let that be the one that hands you a slow breath on the way out.
>
> Same app. Same feed. You just come away better than you went in.

**Headline:** The problem isn't the scroll. It's how it makes you feel.

**Description:** Start with one app.

**Alt hooks to rotate**

- Cold turkey never lasted, and it was never going to.
- You don't need fixing. (retains the earlier stance framing if you want to keep testing it)

*Hook note: "the problem isn't the scroll, it's how the scroll makes you feel" is the positioning brief's central argument — quantity versus state — said in plain language. It's the sharpest sentence in the whole set and probably belongs on the homepage regardless of which cell wins.*

*Note the earlier draft opened "the problem was never how long you scrolled," which is a harder claim than it needs to be: for plenty of people duration genuinely is part of the problem, and denying that outright loses them in the first line. "The problem isn't the scroll, it's how the scroll makes you feel" redirects instead of contradicting — nobody has to disagree with it to keep reading.*

*Two deliberate changes from the raw draft:*

*1. **Cold turkey moved out of the hook.** Opening on "quitting cold turkey doesn't work" makes C and B both start from "the thing you already tried failed," which collapses them back into one cell. Leading on the reframe keeps B as the anti-lockout argument and gives C the distinct claim: everyone in this category is measuring the wrong variable. Cold turkey still appears — just as support, not as the opening.*

*2. **Clinical terms removed.** "Anxiety" and "depression" are cut deliberately, for two separate reasons. Meta's personal attributes policy prohibits ad copy that implies knowledge of the viewer's mental health, and "the app that makes you feel the worst — anxiety, depression" reads directly into that; it risks rejection at review. Separately, naming depression next to a breathing app implies a treatment claim, which is the kind of thing that draws scrutiny on App Store review too. "Restless, behind, left out" carries the same recognition with none of the exposure. FOMO and "lesser than others" were fine — they survive as "behind" and "left out."*

## Running it

**Hold everything else constant.** Same creative, same audience, same landing page across all three. If the image or targeting moves with the copy, you'll learn nothing you can carry to the website.

**Length is now a second variable.** Running short, medium and long means a win is a win for angle *and* length together — you won't be able to fully separate them from one test. That's an acceptable trade for a first read, and the variety keeps the set from looking like three versions of one ad. If a cell wins decisively and you want to know which factor did it, the clean follow-up is to re-run the winning angle at all three lengths.

**Don't judge on CTR.** This is the part that matters most given what you're using the result for. Cell B will probably win clicks — it names a familiar category and promises a reversal, which is inherently clickable. That doesn't make it the right homepage headline; a visitor arriving from B has only been told what DeRot *isn't*, which is a fine ad and a weak hero section.

Rank on the furthest-down metric you can measure at this volume. Install-to-first-breath is ideal. Install-to-activate or trial start both work.

**Expect a split.** The likeliest outcome is B winning traffic and A or C winning activation. That's a useful result, not a failed test — it would mean the ad layer should lead with contrast while the site leads with symptom or stance. Ads and homepage don't have to give the same answer.

**On volume.** Three cells needs roughly 50% more budget than two for the same confidence per cell. If spend is tight, drop C and run A against B — the stance angle is the one most recoverable later from organic and Reddit, where testing it costs nothing.

## Checked against `DeRot-Copy-Rules.md`

**Compliant:** no physiology or biology jargon; no "use your phone less"; no "breathe before you open" — the trigger is always described as pausing you mid-scroll; "lock" used only in the negative ("nothing gets locked"), never as something DeRot does.

**Two things to resolve before these run.**

**1. Em-dashes.** The copy rules say shipped copy avoids them in favour of commas, colons and periods. All three cells currently use them, including inside the breath counts ("Breathe in — 1, 2, 3"). Needs a pass before anything goes live. The counts are the awkward case: a colon reads as a label ("Breathe in: 1, 2, 3") which may actually be clearer, but it's a different rhythm and worth Clayton's eye.

**2. Cell B's headline.** "Locking yourself out didn't work. Try the opposite." The rules put "Not another screen time blocker" in the *not this* column, preferring the positive form "Never locked out of your apps." B's body copy is fine — it describes the reader's own lockout experience, which is accurate use of "lock" — but the headline makes the same against-the-competition move the rules steer away from. Judgment call: B's entire angle is the contrast, so either the headline softens or the rules get an explicit carve-out for paid acquisition.

*Note: the positioning brief's physiology framing ("it's not woo, it's physiology") is already retired by `DeRot-Copy-Rules.md`, which supersedes it. No conflict to resolve there.*
