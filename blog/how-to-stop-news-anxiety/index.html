How to Stop News Anxiety Without Tuning Out of the World

Staying informed isn't the problem. The endless headline feed is. Here's why it keeps you on alert and how to come back down mid-scroll.

By DeRot Editorial · November 18, 2026 · 10 min read

Key takeaways

  • To stop news anxiety, change how the news reaches you rather than whether you follow it: fewer alerts, set check-in times, and sources that end.
  • Headlines keep your body on alert because a feed delivers one threat after another without context, and research links repeated coverage to higher acute stress.
  • The urge to keep checking is a known loop: people consume more news to ease the feeling of threat, which researchers describe as a "vicious cycle" that draws them further in.
  • A quick mid-scroll reset helps: drop your jaw and shoulders, take 3 to 5 slow breaths (inhale 4, exhale 6) or one or two physiological sighs, then decide on purpose what to do next.
  • Headline stress disorder is a popular label coined by psychologist Steven Stosny, not a clinical diagnosis, and most research on news and distress is correlational.

To stop news anxiety, you usually don't need to stop following the news. The trouble is the delivery: alarming headlines arriving one after another in a feed that never ends, so your body stays on alert long after you have the facts. Reset mid-scroll with a few slow breaths, then change how the news reaches you.

If this sounds familiar, you have a lot of company. In the American Psychological Association's 2017 Stress in America survey, 95% of US adults said they follow the news regularly and 56% said doing so causes them stress. The same survey found that 72% believe the media blows things out of proportion. Feeling rattled by the news is a common reaction to the way it reaches you. It isn't a personal failing, and it doesn't mean you should care less about the world.

Why do headlines put your body on alert?

Headlines put your body on alert because they are written to grab attention, and in a feed you get threat after threat with the context stripped away. Each one registers as something to watch, and nothing in the feed ever signals that the danger has passed.

A news article has a beginning, a middle, and an end. A headline feed has none of those. Wildfire, layoffs, an election poll, a new virus variant, a war, all within one thumb-swipe, each phrased to make you stop. Taking in the facts might take two minutes. The alarm takes longer to wear off.

Research suggests that the repetition matters. In a survey of 4,675 US adults after the 2013 Boston Marathon bombings, people who took in six or more hours a day of bombing-related coverage reported more acute stress than people who were directly exposed to the event. Those heavy consumers were about nine times as likely to report high acute stress as people who saw little coverage. The authors suggest that repeated media exposure may keep acute stress going well after the event itself. It was a single survey taken at one point in time, and stressed people may also seek out more coverage, so the arrow could point both ways. The pattern still deserves attention.

The news doesn't have to be dramatic to affect you, either. In a 10-day smartphone study of 63 Dutch adults, news that people perceived as more negative was linked to worse mood in the moment, and the effect was stronger when the story felt personally relevant. Ordinary daily news, the kind you skim on a lunch break, was enough.

In your body it tends to look like this: jaw clenched, shoulders creeping toward your ears, breath shallow and high in the chest, a tight band across the ribs. You feel wired and tired at once. We go deeper on that physical side in why scrolling makes you anxious.

Why do you keep checking the news when it makes you feel worse?

You keep checking because checking feels like it will settle the threat, even when it does the opposite. Researchers describe it as a loop: consuming more news to ease the sense of threat, which tends to keep the threat feeling active.

A 2022 study led by Bryan McLaughlin described how some people slip into a "constant state of high alert" while following the news. Instead of tuning out, they get caught in what McLaughlin calls a "vicious cycle," drawn further in and checking for updates to calm the very feeling the updates keep stirring up.

That loop explains the 20-minutes-later feeling. You open the app to check one story. The story is unresolved (most big stories are), so you look for the next update, then a related headline, then a reaction to it. The infinite scroll never offers a natural stopping point, so the session ends when you happen to look up, usually more tense than when you started. That comes from how the feed works, not from a flaw in your character.

One small counter-move: read past the headline. The full story usually includes the context a headline leaves out, and that context is often what lets the alarm settle.

What is headline stress disorder? (A popular label, not a diagnosis)

Headline stress disorder is an informal term coined by psychologist Steven Stosny to describe a strong emotional reaction to the news, such as anxiety, tension, or dread that builds as you take in headlines. It is not a clinical diagnosis, and no doctor will write it on a chart.

The label caught on because it named a feeling lots of people recognized: the background tension of continual alerts and headlines, with no clear point where the news is "done."

Use it as shorthand for an experience, not as a way to diagnose yourself. If news-related distress is persistent or affecting your daily life, the support section below matters more than any label.

How to stop news anxiety mid-scroll: a 60-second reset

The best time to use a calming technique is the moment you notice you're wound up, which is usually partway through a news scroll, not before you open the app. This reset takes about a minute and has no breath holds.

  1. Notice your jaw and shoulders. Unclench your teeth so there's a small gap between them. Let your shoulders drop an inch. Put the phone down or turn it face-down if you can.
  2. Breathe slowly with a longer exhale. Do 3 to 5 Calm breaths: inhale through your nose for 4 counts, then exhale gently for 6 counts. Or do one or two physiological sighs: a full inhale through the nose, a short top-up inhale, then a long, slow exhale through the mouth.
  3. Choose on purpose. Ask yourself what you actually need right now. Keep reading, switch from the feed to one full article, or close the app. Any of the three is fine. What matters is that you're deciding, not the feed.

What it should feel like

By the third or fourth slow exhale, most people notice their shoulders settle a little and their breathing move lower, toward the belly. The urgency doesn't vanish, but it usually softens enough that you can think about the news instead of just reacting to it. Common mistakes

  • Forcing the exhale. The 6-count exhale should feel like a slow sigh, not like blowing out birthday candles. If 4-6 feels like a strain, try 3-5.
  • Stacking rapid sighs. One or two physiological sighs is plenty. Doing many fast ones in a row can tip into overbreathing and make you lightheaded.
  • Treating it as a test. You don't have to feel fully calm afterward. A slightly lower baseline is a good result.

What the evidence does (and doesn't) say about this reset

The best-known study here is a 2023 randomized trial of 108 people who practiced 5 minutes a day for 28 days. Cyclic sighing (repeated physiological sighs with long exhales) produced the largest gain in positive mood and lowered breathing rate more than mindfulness meditation. State anxiety also fell, but no group differed significantly from the others on that measure, including mindfulness. The sample was fairly small.

So a minute of slow breathing mid-scroll is a practical adaptation of that research, not a tested dose. It's a reasonable, low-risk way to help you feel calmer in the moment, and you can find out quickly whether it works for you. For more on why a longer exhale matters, see our 4-6 breathing guide.

Kinder news habits that keep you informed

The habits that help most change how the news reaches you, not how much you care. A few small, durable changes:

  • Set check-in times. Pick two or three times a day, such as mid-morning and early evening, to catch up. Outside those windows, the news will still be there.
  • Turn off breaking-news alerts. Alerts push the alarm to you at random moments. Most stories don't need you within the minute. Keep alerts for the one or two topics that genuinely affect your safety or plans.
  • Read instead of watching autoplay clips. Autoplay video keeps rolling into the next clip and the next set of images. The research doesn't prove video is worse than text, but reading lets you set the pace and stop when you've got the point.
  • Follow a few trusted sources that end. A morning newsletter or a single outlet's front page has a last item. The feed doesn't. Swapping the endless feed for two or three sources gives your brain a finish line.
  • Read past the headline. The full piece usually adds context the headline left out, and context is often what lets your body stand down.
  • End on one thing you can do. Donate, check on someone, sign up to volunteer, make a plan, or simply decide what you'll do with this information tomorrow. Finishing on an action gives the alarm somewhere to go.

Why a "news detox" isn't the answer

A news detox treats you as the problem and cutting yourself off as the cure, when the real issue is the delivery. It can also backfire. Swearing off the news can end with a binge when something big happens, and the uncertainty you've been avoiding can make the catch-up session feel even more urgent.

Short breaks are fine, and sometimes wise. After a traumatic event, especially one that touches you directly, stepping away from repeated graphic coverage for a while is a sensible choice.

Plenty of people are already stepping back. The Reuters Institute's 2025 survey of nearly 100,000 people in 48 markets found selective news avoidance at an all-time high (full report). That makes sense as a reaction, but tuning out completely leaves you less informed and doesn't change the feed waiting when you return. The aim here is to take in news in a way your body can handle, not to stop.

What the research on news and anxiety does and doesn't show

The research shows a consistent link between heavy or compulsive news consumption and distress, plus short-term mood dips from negative news. It does not show that the news causes anxiety disorders, and most of it can't say which way the arrow points.

Study Who What it found What it can't tell us
Holman et al., 2014 4,675 US adults after the Boston bombings 6+ hours a day of coverage linked to more acute stress than direct exposure Whether coverage caused stress or stressed people watched more
McLaughlin et al., 2022 About 1,100 US adults 16.5% had severely problematic news use; 73.6% of them reported mental ill-being vs 8% of others Cause and effect (measured at one time point)
de Hoog & Verboon, 2020 63 Dutch adults, 10 days Negative news linked to worse mood in the moment, more so when personally relevant Long-term effects on mental health
Shabahang et al., 2024 800 students in Iran and the US Doomscrolling moderately associated with existential anxiety Whether doomscrolling causes existential dread

The McLaughlin figures are striking: among people with severely problematic news consumption, 73.6% reported mental ill-being and 61% reported physical ill-being, compared with 8% and 6.1% of other participants. But the survey captured a single moment, so it's just as possible that people who already feel unwell are drawn to more news.

The same caution applies to the link between doomscrolling and existential anxiety, the sense that life feels fragile or meaningless after a long scroll. An independent summary from the British Psychological Society describes that association as moderate and correlational. It's a real pattern worth taking seriously, but it isn't proof of cause.

The honest takeaway: negative news can dip your mood in the moment, heavy repeated exposure is associated with more stress, and the checking loop is a recognized pattern. That's enough reason to change how the news reaches you, without overstating what the science shows.

When news anxiety needs more support

Breathing and better habits can help you feel calmer in the moment, but they don't treat anxiety disorders, PTSD, or insomnia. If news-related worry persists, disrupts your sleep or eating, or feels like more than you can handle, talk to a doctor or mental health professional.

If you were directly affected by a traumatic event, or someone close to you was, it's reasonable to limit repeated graphic coverage of it and to reach out for support early rather than waiting it out.

If you're in crisis or thinking about harming yourself, call or text 988 in the US to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, or contact your local emergency services.

Frequently asked questions

Is it OK to stop watching the news for my mental health?

Yes, a short break is fine, especially after a traumatic event or a run of distressing coverage. The catch is that quitting completely can end in a catch-up binge when something big happens. For most people, changing how the news reaches you works better over time: fewer alerts, set check-in times, and a couple of trusted sources that have an end, so you stay informed without the constant alarm.

Is reading the news better than watching it?

For many people, yes, though research hasn't proven that video is worse than text. Reading lets you set the pace, skim past graphic details, and stop when you have the point. Autoplay clips keep rolling into the next story and the next set of images. If video tends to leave you more wound up, try switching to written articles or a daily newsletter for a week and notice how you feel afterward.

Should I avoid news before bed?

If late-night headlines leave you wired, it's worth moving your last news check earlier in the evening. Unresolved stories tend to keep the mind turning them over, which isn't what you want right before sleep. Try finishing your news for the day an hour or two before bed, ending on one thing you can do about it, and doing a few slow breaths with a longer exhale if you still feel keyed up.

Can doomscrolling cause existential anxiety or dread?

Research has found a link, but not proof of cause. A 2024 study of 800 students in Iran and the US found doomscrolling was moderately associated with existential anxiety, the sense that life feels fragile or meaningless. The study was correlational, so people who already feel that way may also scroll more. If a long news scroll leaves you with that heavy feeling, it's a sign to change the format and timing of your news.

How do I stay informed without doomscrolling?

Swap the endless feed for sources that end. Pick two or three trusted outlets or a daily newsletter, check them at set times, and turn off breaking-news alerts except for topics that affect your safety. Read past the headline for context, and finish each session on something you can actually do. If you notice tension building mid-scroll, take three to five slow breaths before deciding whether to keep going.
