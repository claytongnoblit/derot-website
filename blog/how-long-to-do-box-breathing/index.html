How Long Should You Do Box Breathing For? From 1 Minute to 5

The short answer is about a minute. Here's how many rounds to do in each situation, how to tell when you can stop, and what the research shows.

By DeRot Editorial · October 23, 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • One round of 4-4-4-4 box breathing takes 16 seconds, so four rounds is about one minute and five minutes is about 18 to 19 rounds.
  • In a 2026 preregistered pilot of 47 students, one minute of box breathing during real anxious moments reduced state anxiety more than a passive control, measured five minutes later.
  • In the 2023 Stanford trial of 5 minutes a day for 28 days, mood gains across the breathing groups grew with the number of days practiced, but box breathing did not significantly beat mindfulness and HRV did not change.
  • You can stop once your breath slows on its own and your shoulders drop. More minutes are not automatically better.
  • If 4-second holds feel like too much, switch to 3-3-3-3 or 2-2-2-2, or skip the hold after the exhale. Stop if you feel dizzy or lightheaded.

How long should you do box breathing for? For a quick reset, four rounds of 4-4-4-4 is enough, and that takes about one minute. For a fuller reset, aim for about five minutes. As a habit, once or twice a day is a common recommendation. Short sessions count.

Most guides bury a single number ("a few minutes," "three to four rounds") somewhere near the bottom. The more useful answer depends on why you're doing it, so below we break it down by situation, then cover the research and how to tell when you can stop.

The quick math: how long one round of box breathing takes

Box breathing is a paced breathing technique: you breathe in for a count of four, hold for four, breathe out for four and hold again for four, tracing the four sides of a box. One trip around the box is one round.

At one second per count, one round takes 16 seconds. That makes the numbers simple:

  • 4 rounds: about 64 seconds
  • 8 rounds: about 2 minutes
  • 18 to 19 rounds: about 5 minutes

How many rounds of box breathing should you do?

Four rounds is the most common answer, and it's a good default for calming down in the moment. Cleveland Clinic suggests three to four rounds, and both University of Minnesota Extension and RWJBarnabas Health suggest four. A University of Michigan handout goes a bit longer, suggesting at least five boxes, and says you can start with a count of 2 or 3.

Situation Rounds (4-4-4-4) Time What it's for
Mid-scroll reset 4 About 1 minute Taking the edge off after a feed has wound you up
Fuller reset 18 to 19 About 5 minutes When one minute took the edge off but you want to settle further
Daily practice 4 to 19 1 to 5 minutes, once or twice a day Making the technique familiar so it's easy to reach for
Winding down at night 4 to 8, with gentler counts 1 to 2 minutes A quiet transition toward bed

These sources don't really disagree. Three or four rounds is sized for a single stressful moment, and five or more minutes is closer to a practice session. None of these numbers come from a trial that compared different lengths of box breathing, so treat them as sensible starting points rather than prescribed doses.

Is 1 minute of box breathing enough?

For taking the edge off a stressful moment, one minute may well be enough. In a 2026 preregistered pilot study of 47 students, one minute of box breathing during real anxious moments in daily life reduced state anxiety more than a passive control, measured five minutes later. One minute of cyclic sighing did too.

Keep that result in proportion, though. It's a small pilot study in students, and what we can report comes from the study's abstract. It suggests a short session can help in the moment. It doesn't show that one minute is the ideal length, or that it works for everyone.

The same study also found slower reaction times after both breathing exercises. The authors called this unexpected, and box breathing was also linked to fewer inhibition errors, especially in participants with higher trait anxiety. Either way, it's sensible to give yourself a short pause before anything that needs quick reactions, such as driving.

Box breathing mid-scroll: a 60-second script

Here's the moment we have in mind. You picked up your phone to check one thing. Twenty minutes later your jaw is tight, your shoulders are up near your ears, and you feel oddly wired for someone who has been sitting still. That's the feed doing what feeds do, and it's a good time for four rounds. (We explain why it happens in nervous system overload from your phone.)

  1. Put the phone face down, or turn the screen off.
  2. Breathe out fully so you start the box empty.
  3. Breathe in gently through your nose for 4.
  4. Hold for 4, keeping your throat and shoulders soft rather than clamped.
  5. Breathe out slowly for 4.
  6. Hold, empty, for 4.
  7. Repeat for four rounds. Tap one finger against your thumb at the end of each round so you don't have to keep count in your head.
  8. Let your breathing go back to normal for a couple of breaths and notice how you feel.

That's it. You can stop at four rounds. If you'd like something to follow, open the box breathing timer and stop it after about a minute.

What does a 5-minute session do? What the Stanford study found

The best-known study of box breathing used five-minute sessions: a 2023 Stanford randomized trial in which 108 people spent five minutes a day for 28 days on mindfulness meditation or on one of several breathing practices, including box breathing and cyclic sighing. Twenty-one people were assigned to box breathing.

Here's what it found:

  • Positive mood went up in the box breathing group, but only cyclic sighing significantly beat mindfulness on positive mood.
  • State anxiety fell in every group, including mindfulness, with no significant differences between groups.
  • Across the breathing groups as a whole, gains in positive mood grew with the number of days people practiced. The paper doesn't report this for box breathing on its own.
  • There was no significant change in heart rate variability (HRV) or resting heart rate.

On HRV, the broader picture is more encouraging. A 2022 review of 223 studies found that slow breathing in general increased vagally mediated HRV during sessions, right after them, and over multiple sessions. That hasn't been shown for box breathing specifically, so we wouldn't promise it.

The Stanford study was exploratory, and each technique had a small group. Its results suggest that five minutes of daily box breathing may lift mood and ease anxiety without clearly beating mindfulness meditation, and that across the breathing groups, practicing on more days went with bigger mood gains. That's a long way from headlines about a five-minute breathing trick to "kill stress instantly". The evidence describes a modest, gentle effect, not an off switch.

How do you know when you can stop?

You can stop once the edge is gone, even if that's before your planned number of rounds. We haven't found research that pins down a stopping point for box breathing, so these cues come from experience rather than studies:

  • Your breath starts to slow and deepen on its own, and the counts feel easy rather than effortful.
  • Your jaw unclenches and your shoulders drop.
  • The urge to keep counting fades, and you can think about what's next without a jolt.

If you've done four rounds and still feel wound up, do four more, or switch to a pattern with a longer exhale. Beyond that, more minutes aren't automatically better. Box breathing is a way back to baseline, not an endurance event.

What if 4-second holds feel like too much?

Shorten the counts. Box breathing works at whatever length lets you breathe without strain. Four seconds is a convention, not a rule.

  • 3-3-3-3: 12-second rounds, five rounds a minute.
  • 2-2-2-2: 8-second rounds, a good place to start if holds make you uneasy.
  • Skip the empty hold: breathe in for 4, hold for 4, breathe out for 4, then go straight into the next inhale. Many people find the hold after the exhale the hardest part.

This matches both the research and the clinical advice. In the Stanford trial, box counts were personalized, from about 3 to 4 seconds up to 8 to 10 seconds, and participants were told to shorten them if they felt strained. Cleveland Clinic says to breathe gently, without straining, and notes that the counts can lengthen with practice. You can lengthen them over time if you enjoy it, but you don't have to.

If holds don't suit you at all, try calm 4-6 breathing: in for 4, out for 6, with no holds. Our guide to 4-6 breathing walks you through it. For a 3-3-3-3 or 2-2-2-2 box, the custom breathing timer lets you set your own pattern.

How often should you do box breathing?

Once or twice a day is a reasonable rhythm, plus any time you need it. Cleveland Clinic recommends one or two times a day regardless of how you feel, which keeps the technique familiar for when you need it.

The Stanford data also shows what a realistic habit looks like. Breathwork participants averaged 5.76 minutes a day on 19.6 of the 28 days. They missed some days, and their mood gains still grew with the number of days they practiced. So a few minutes on most days is a realistic target. The study didn't compare that with occasional longer sessions.

At bedtime, keep it gentle: shorter counts, a dim room, and a few minutes at most. Some people find the holds a little alerting at night and prefer a longer exhale instead. If scrolling is what keeps you up, doomscrolling at night covers that side of it. If you have ongoing trouble sleeping, talk to a doctor.

When to stop: dizziness and other safety notes

Box breathing is gentle for most people, but a few rules matter.

  • Stop if you feel dizzy or lightheaded. Go back to normal breathing and sit or lie down. Next time, shorten the counts or skip the hold after the exhale.
  • Keep it slow and soft. Don't force your breath or speed up the pattern.
  • Check with a clinician first if you're pregnant or have a heart or lung condition, seizures, glaucoma, a history of fainting, or panic that gets worse when you focus on your breathing.
  • Never hold your breath in or near water, or while driving.
  • Pause before tasks that need quick reactions, since reaction times may be slower right after a session.
  • It isn't a treatment. Box breathing can help you feel calmer, but it doesn't treat anxiety, panic or insomnia. If stress or anxiety is persistent or getting in the way of your life, talk to a professional. If you're in crisis, call or text 988 in the US, or contact your local emergency services.

Frequently asked questions

How fast does box breathing work?

Many people notice a small shift within a minute or two: their breath slows and their shoulders loosen. In a 2026 pilot study of 47 students, one minute of box breathing during anxious moments reduced state anxiety more than a passive control, measured five minutes later. It's a small study, so treat it as encouraging rather than proof. In the 2023 Stanford trial, mood gains across the breathing groups grew with the number of days people practiced over four weeks.

Can you do box breathing for too long, and why do I feel dizzy?

There's no set maximum, but longer isn't automatically better, and long holds or forceful breathing can leave some people dizzy or lightheaded. That usually means the counts are too long or you're breathing too hard. Stop, breathe normally and sit down. Next time, try 3-3-3-3 or 2-2-2-2, or skip the hold after the exhale. If the dizziness keeps happening, check with a clinician.

Does box breathing raise HRV?

Not clearly. In the 2023 Stanford trial, five minutes a day for 28 days led to no significant change in heart rate variability or resting heart rate. A 2022 review of 223 studies found that slow breathing in general increases vagally mediated HRV during and after sessions, but that hasn't been shown for box breathing specifically. The more consistent findings are about mood and feeling calmer.

Is box breathing better than cyclic sighing or 4-7-8?

No technique is clearly best for everyone. In the Stanford trial, cyclic sighing was the only practice that significantly beat mindfulness on positive mood, and box breathing did not. In a 2026 pilot, one minute of either box breathing or cyclic sighing reduced anxiety more than doing nothing. 4-7-8 uses a longer hold and a longer exhale. Pick the one that feels comfortable and that you'll actually use.

Is box breathing good before sleep?

It can be a calm way to wind down, but there's no strong evidence that it treats sleep problems. At night, keep the counts short and gentle and stop after a few minutes. Some people find the holds a little alerting and prefer a pattern with a longer exhale, such as breathing in for 4 and out for 6. If you have ongoing trouble sleeping, talk to a doctor.

Should I lengthen the count over time?

Only if you want to. Cleveland Clinic notes that the counts can lengthen with practice, and in the Stanford trial counts were personalized, ranging from 3 to 4 seconds up to 8 to 10 seconds. But longer isn't better by default. The right count is the one you can keep up without straining, gasping or feeling lightheaded. Plenty of people stay at a count of 4, or 3, indefinitely.
