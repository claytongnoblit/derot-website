Clearspace Alternatives: Pause Apps, Blockers, or a Mid-Scroll Reset

A neutral, dated comparison of the apps people try after Clearspace, sorted by when each one steps in, how strict it gets, and what it costs.

By DeRot Editorial · November 24, 2026 · 11 min read

Key takeaways

  • For a door pause like Clearspace's, the closest alternatives are one sec and ScreenZen, and ScreenZen is completely free with optional tips (App Store, checked 24 Nov 2026).
  • For firm limits without installing anything, Apple Screen Time with App Limits, Downtime and a passcode is the strictest option on iPhone.
  • Google Pause Point is an Android feature with a 10-second pause. It is not available on iPhone.
  • In a PNAS experiment on the friction app one sec, the option to dismiss did most of the work and the deliberation message alone had no significant effect, which helps explain why intention prompts get tapped through.
  • Door pauses decide whether you open an app and time limits decide how long you stay. Neither is built to bring you back down when the feed has wound you up 15 minutes in.

What Clearspace does, and why people look for something else

Clearspace is an iPhone app that puts a pause in front of the apps you choose, then asks whether you want to continue. According to its App Store listing, the pause asks you to proceed after a deep breath or opt out, and the app adds blocking and limits, schedules, challenges, accountability partners, and streaks. It requires iOS 16 or later.

The Clearspace website describes two ways to earn more scrolling time: Push-Up to Scroll and Breathe to Scroll. It also mentions app limits, a pause that shows how many uses you have left for the day, friend challenges, and focus schedules.

On price, Clearspace is a free download with in-app purchases. The US App Store listing shows Premium at $6.99/month or $44.99/year, plus a Family option at $79.99 (billing period not stated in the listing), checked 24 November 2026.

Clearspace works well for plenty of people. The reasons people go looking for an alternative tend to be practical:

  • The pause became a tap-through. After a few weeks, the deep breath turns into a thumb reflex and you are in the app before you have read the screen.
  • You want it stricter. A pause you can always skip is not the same as a limit you cannot.
  • You want it free. Some alternatives cost nothing.
  • You want another platform. Clearspace is iPhone only, and some people scroll just as much on a Mac.
  • Earning minutes does not suit you. Some people enjoy the push-up mechanic. Others find it turns their phone into a points system they did not ask for.
  • You still come out of a scroll wound up. This one gets the least attention, and we come back to it below.

How we compared Clearspace alternatives

We compared each alternative on four criteria:

  1. When it steps in. At the door (before the app opens), by time (a limit or schedule), or mid-session (a time-out or reset once you are already scrolling).
  2. How strict it is. Whether you can always skip it, or whether it can hold firm.
  3. What it costs. Free tier, paid tier, and the listed price with currency and billing period.
  4. Platforms. Where it runs, and the minimum OS version where stated.

Every fact comes from the app's own App Store listing, website, or support page, checked 24 November 2026. We did not assign star ratings, and we are not claiming hands-on testing. Where an app does not state something, we say so.

The alternatives, app by app

one sec

Best for: people who like Clearspace's door pause and want something similar that also works on a Mac.

How it works: one sec delays access instead of blocking it. When you open a chosen app, you get a short pause first, which can include a breathing animation set up through Apple's Shortcuts app. You can write custom intentions, and a feature called Re-Intervention removes you from an app after a time you set.

What stands out: it is the app studied in a peer-reviewed PNAS paper on friction (more on that below). It also offers strict, scheduled, or manual blocks when a pause is not enough, and it runs on iOS 15 or later and macOS 14 or later.

Drawbacks: the free tier covers one app. The breathing animation depends on a Shortcuts automation, which is one more thing to set up.

Price: free for one app. Pro pricing varies by region. On the Malaysian App Store listing, Pro was RM16.99/month, RM49.90/year, or RM399 lifetime (checked 24 November 2026). We did not verify US prices for this page, so check the listing in your own store. If you already use one sec and want options beyond it, see our one sec alternatives comparison.

ScreenZen

Best for: people who want a pause like Clearspace's that is free and can get stricter.

How it works: ScreenZen adds a delay before an app opens that can grow with each open, with breathing during the wait. You can choose to open an app for a short set period, set daily goals, turn on strict blocks, and use a settings lock with an accountability partner.

What stands out: the price. The ScreenZen website says "Less screen time. No subscription." and describes the app as donation-supported and completely free. It blocks websites as well as apps.

Drawbacks: a delay that grows with every open is the point of the design, but it can feel like a slow toll booth on a day when you need an app quickly. It needs iOS or iPadOS 16.4 or later.

Price: free, with optional tips of $5, $10, $20, or $40 listed on the App Store (checked 24 November 2026). It runs on iPhone, iPad, and Mac (macOS 13 or later), and the website footer also lists Android and Windows. We cover it in more depth in our ScreenZen alternatives guide.

Apple Screen Time

Best for: people who want firm limits and would rather not install another app.

How it works: Screen Time is built into iOS. Per Apple Support, App Limits can apply to categories or individual apps, Downtime blocks apps and notifications on a schedule, and a passcode can protect the settings.

What stands out: it is the strictest option here without adding anything. If you hand the passcode to a partner or friend, the limit stops being a suggestion.

Drawbacks: there is no pause, prompt, or breathing step. It is a limit, not a nudge. If you set the passcode yourself, you also know how to get around it.

Price: free with iOS.

Google Pause Point (Android only)

Best for: Android users who want a built-in pause before distracting apps.

How it works: Google describes Pause Point as a 10-second breather that asks "Why am I here?" and offers breathing, a timer, photos, or suggested alternative apps. TechCrunch reports that it applies to apps you have labeled as distractions.

What stands out: turning it off requires a restart, which is a clever bit of friction aimed at the moment you are most tempted to disable it.

Drawbacks: it is not on iPhone, and device availability on Android may be limited.

Price: free with Android.

Do pause-before-opening apps actually work?

Pause-before-opening apps can reduce how often people open target apps, but the evidence is early and the reason they work is less obvious than it looks. The best-known study is of one sec, published in PNAS in 2023.

In the six-week field study, openings of the target apps fell 57% among 280 analyzed users (719 were recruited), and about 36% of opening attempts were dismissed. That is a meaningful number. It also comes with real caveats: the field study had no control group, users chose to install the app themselves, many dropped out before analysis, and the app's creator is a co-author.

The more revealing part is the preregistered experiment with 500 participants, run online with a video-watching task rather than on people's own phones. The option to dismiss (to simply close the app) had the strongest effect. The 10-second delay added nothing beyond dismissal, and the deliberation message on its own had no significant effect.

That finding matters if Clearspace's prompt turned into autopilot for you. The intention question was never the active ingredient. What helped was a clear, easy exit at the moment of opening. Tapping through a prompt is a predictable result of the design, not a personal failing. (Our glossary has a short definition of friction in app design if the term is new.)

There is also the question of how people use these tools over time. A 2024 in-the-wild study of design frictions, based on usage data from about 1,000 one sec users, found that people take periodic breaks from these interventions and, when they come back, quickly recover from a pattern of overuse. It is observational data from one app's users, so treat it as a signal rather than a settled result.

One last point: the 57% figure counts app openings. It does not measure how people felt during or after the sessions they did open.

Is there a Google Pause Point for iPhone?

No. Google Pause Point is an Android feature, and Google's announcement does not mention iPhone. one sec's own blog says Pause Point is not on iPhone or iPad and is limited to certain Pixel phones. That is a vendor claim from a company that sells an alternative, so check your own device.

The nearest iPhone equivalents are one sec (which, per its blog, also pauses for 10 seconds), ScreenZen, and Clearspace's own pause.

Worth keeping in mind: a 10-second door pause helps you decide whether to open an app. It does not change how you feel 15 minutes into a session you did decide to open.

Which Clearspace alternative should you pick?

  • If you want a similar door pause that also works on your Mac, pick one sec.
  • If you want it free and a bit stricter, with a pause that grows each time, pick ScreenZen.
  • If you want firm limits and nothing new to install, use Apple Screen Time with a passcode, ideally one someone else sets.
  • If you want a hard block that is difficult to skip, use Screen Time with a shared passcode or ScreenZen's strict blocks.
  • If you are on Android, try Pause Point.
  • If opening the app was never the problem, and the trouble is how you feel partway through, read the next section.

What about the wired feeling mid-scroll?

Most screen-time apps answer two questions: should you open this app, and how long should you stay. Neither is "how do you feel right now?" That is the gap a lot of people are pointing at when they search for a mindful scroll app.

The feeling has a shape. You open an app to check one thing. Fifteen minutes later your jaw is set, your shoulders have crept up, your breathing is short and high in the chest, and you feel vaguely wired. A door pause already happened (you passed it). A time limit has not fired yet. The feed, meanwhile, keeps going, which is rather the point of the feed.

For that moment, a short slow-exhale reset is a better fit than another prompt. Here is one you can do without leaving your seat:

  1. Put the phone face down or rest it on your leg.
  2. Drop your shoulders and unclench your jaw. Let your tongue rest.
  3. Breathe in gently through your nose for a count of 4.
  4. Breathe out slowly, through your nose or softly through your mouth, for a count of 6.
  5. Repeat. Three rounds take about 30 seconds; five or six rounds take about a minute.
  6. Notice one physical change (warmer hands, looser shoulders, a quieter head) before you decide what to do next.

If you prefer a guide, the free calm 4-6 breathing timer paces it for you. Another option is cyclic sighing: two inhales through the nose, the second one a short top-up, then a long, slow exhale. The physiological sigh timer walks you through it.

Why the long exhale? Stanford Medicine's summary explains the proposed mechanism: exhalation engages the parasympathetic nervous system, the calming branch that can slow heart rate. Heart rate itself did not change in any group in the study below, so its findings are about mood and breathing rather than heart rate. In a 2023 randomized study of 108 adults, five minutes a day of cyclic sighing for 28 days raised positive mood more than mindfulness meditation did. State anxiety fell in all groups, and breathing rate slowed more with cyclic sighing than with mindfulness. It was an exploratory study in adults without moderate-to-severe psychiatric or medical conditions, mostly university students, so read it as encouraging rather than definitive. For more on coming down once you are already overloaded, see how to come down mid-scroll.

A few safety notes. Keep the breathing slow and gentle. If you feel dizzy, lightheaded, or tingly, stop and breathe normally. Breath holds and fast breathing are not for everyone, including people who are pregnant or have heart or lung conditions, or a history of fainting or panic. Breathing can help you feel calmer, but it is not a treatment. If scrolling is tied to persistent anxiety, low mood, or sleep problems, talk to a professional. In a crisis, contact local emergency services, or call or text 988 in the US.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a Google Pause Point for iPhone?

No. Pause Point is an Android feature that adds a 10-second pause before apps you label as distractions, and Google's announcement does not mention iPhone. On iPhone, the closest equivalents are one sec and ScreenZen, which both add a delay before an app opens, or Clearspace's own pause. Apple Screen Time offers limits and schedules but no pause step.

What's the difference between Clearspace and one sec?

Both add a pause before you open chosen apps. Clearspace leans into challenges, streaks, accountability partners, and earning minutes through Push-Up to Scroll or Breathe to Scroll, and it runs on iPhone only. one sec focuses on the delay itself, with custom intentions, a Re-Intervention time-out, and strict or scheduled blocks, and it also runs on Mac. one sec is free for one app.

Is ScreenZen really free?

Yes, according to its own website and App Store listing (checked 24 November 2026). ScreenZen describes itself as donation-supported with no subscription. The App Store lists optional tips of $5, $10, $20, or $40, but the features, including strict blocks and the growing delay, do not require payment. It runs on iPhone, iPad, and Mac, and its site also lists Android and Windows.

How much is Clearspace Premium?

On the US App Store, Clearspace Premium is listed at $6.99 per month or $44.99 per year, with a Family option at $79.99 (billing period not stated in the listing), checked 24 November 2026. The app itself is a free download. Prices can differ by country and change over time, so check the listing in your own App Store before subscribing.

Do pause-before-opening apps work, or do you just tap through them?

Both happen. A PNAS study of one sec found 57% fewer openings of target apps over six weeks, though without a control group. Its experiment showed the easy option to dismiss did most of the work, while the deliberation message alone had no significant effect. That is why intention prompts often become a reflexive tap. A later study found people take periodic breaks from these tools and, when they return, quickly recover from overuse.

Why do I still feel wired after scrolling even with a limit app?

Limit and pause apps manage whether you open an app and how long you stay, not how the feed leaves you feeling. A tense jaw, raised shoulders, and short breathing can build up well before any limit fires. A short slow-exhale reset, such as breathing in for 4 and out for 6 for about 30 seconds, can help you feel calmer. Stop if you feel dizzy.
