4-6 Breathing: How to Do the Calm Pattern in 60 Seconds

In for 4, out for 6, no holds. A gentle breathing pattern you can do in one minute, right when the feed has wound you up.

By DeRot Editorial · October 12, 2026 · 9 min read

Key takeaways

  • 4-6 breathing means breathing in through the nose for 4 counts and out for 6, with no holds, which works out to about 10 seconds per breath and about 6 breaths per minute.
  • Six slow breaths take roughly one minute, so 4-6 breathing fits neatly into a mid-scroll pause when you notice a clenched jaw or a wired feeling.
  • Most mistakes come from trying too hard: gulping the inhale, pushing the exhale, counting too fast, or holding at the top out of habit.
  • If a 6-count exhale feels too long, keep the shape and shorten the counts to 3-5. Comfort matters more than hitting exact numbers.
  • Research most clearly supports the slow pace itself; a longer exhale may add to how relaxed you feel, but it hasn't shown a clear extra benefit over equal breathing.

4-6 breathing is a slow breathing pattern in which you breathe in through your nose for 4 counts and out for 6, with no breath holds. Each breath takes about 10 seconds, so you breathe about 6 times a minute. Six breaths is roughly one minute, which makes it an easy reset right in the middle of a scroll.

It's the pattern to reach for when you surface from a feed with your shoulders up and your head buzzing: nothing to hold, nothing complicated to count, just a slightly longer out-breath than in-breath.

What is 4-6 breathing?

4-6 breathing (sometimes written 4:6, or called extended exhale breathing) is a no-hold pattern: in for a count of 4, out for a count of 6, then straight into the next breath. At roughly one count per second, a full breath takes 10 seconds and the pace lands at about 6 breaths per minute.

That pace is the useful part. Six breaths take about a minute, which fits into the gap between noticing you're wound up and deciding what to do next. A UK mental health guide, Breathing Out Anxiety, recommends exactly this pattern: in for 4, out for 6, without holding your breath.

A few similar names cause confusion:

  • 4-4-6 and 4-2-6 keep the longer out-breath but add a hold of 4 or 2 counts after the inhale. That slows the pace further and gives you one more thing to count.
  • Coherent or resonance breathing (often 5-5) runs at about the same pace, roughly 6 breaths a minute, but the in-breath and out-breath are equal.
  • 4-7-8 is a different pattern entirely, built around a long 7-count hold.

How to do 4-6 breathing, step by step

To do 4-6 breathing, breathe in gently through your nose for 4 counts, then breathe out slowly for 6 counts with no pause in between, and repeat for 6 breaths. Here is the full version:

  1. Get settled. Sit or stand with your shoulders loose and your hands resting. Sit while you're learning, in case you feel lightheaded.
  2. Breathe in softly through your nose for 4. Fill to about 70-80% of a full breath, not to the brim. Let your belly and lower ribs widen rather than lifting your shoulders.
  3. With no pause at the top, breathe out slowly for 6. Use your nose or softly pursed lips, as if cooling a spoonful of soup. Let the air leave; don't squeeze it out.
  4. Let the next inhale start on its own. There may be a tiny natural turnaround at the bottom. Don't turn it into a hold.
  5. Repeat for 6 breaths. That's about one minute.

Count at roughly one per second. "One-and, two-and" is a reliable way to stop the count from speeding up.

Nose or mouth?

Breathe in through your nose; it's quieter and makes a slow inhale easier to control. Breathe out through either. Pursed lips make it easier to stretch the exhale to 6, while breathing out through the nose is less noticeable on a train or in an office.

A 60-second 4-6 breathing script for mid-scroll

The best moment for 4-6 breathing is partway through the feed, when you notice your jaw is set, your shoulders have crept up, or you feel a hum of irritation or a slightly sped-up, wired feeling. You don't have to close the app or decide anything yet. Put the phone face down on your knee, or just look away from the screen.

Breath 1 (0-10 seconds). In, 2, 3, 4. Out, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. Just slow down. If the count wobbles, let it. No judging.

Breaths 2-3 (10-30 seconds). Keep the same count. On each out-breath, let your shoulders drop a little. Don't place them; let them fall.

Breaths 4-5 (30-50 seconds). On the out-breath, unclench your jaw: teeth slightly apart, tongue resting. Soften your eyes and let your gaze widen to the room.

Breath 6 (50-60 seconds). Notice what changed, even if it's small. Then decide on purpose: go back to the feed, answer the message, or put the phone down.

The goal here is your state, not your screen time. You're not trying to log off or earn a streak. If you go back to scrolling a notch calmer, that counts. (If this happens to you mostly in bed, our guide to doomscrolling at night covers that moment specifically.)

What 4-6 breathing feels like (and what it shouldn't)

Done comfortably, 4-6 breathing usually feels like a gradual settling: your breath gets quieter, your shoulders come down, your jaw loosens and the buzzy edge eases over a minute or so. These are common experiences, not guarantees. Some days you'll notice a lot, some days very little, and both are normal.

It shouldn't feel like a strain. Watch for:

  • Air hunger, the sense that you're not getting enough air
  • Gasping or grabbing at the next inhale
  • Tingling in your fingers or lips
  • Lightheadedness or chest discomfort

Any of these means the counts are too long or the breaths too big. Shorten to 3-5, or stop and breathe normally.

Common 4-6 breathing mistakes

Most 4-6 breathing mistakes come from trying too hard. The pattern works best when it feels slightly lazy.

  • Gulping the inhale. Filling to the brim in 4 counts makes the breath sharp. Sip in to about 70-80% instead.
  • Pushing the exhale. Forcing the air out tenses your belly and throat. Let the breath leave slowly, like a tyre with a slow leak.
  • Counting too fast. If your 4-and-6 takes 6 seconds instead of 10, you're at about 10 breaths a minute, right at the cutoff of what researchers define as slow breathing (fewer than 10 a minute). Check against a timer once to calibrate.
  • Lifting the shoulders. Chest-and-shoulder breathing works against the point. Let the belly and lower ribs do the moving.
  • Holding at the top out of habit. If you usually do box or 4-7-8, you may pause automatically. Turn straight from in to out.
  • Big open-mouth sighs. One sigh is fine. Several deep, fast ones in a row can tip you into over-breathing, which is often what causes tingling.
  • Getting perfectionist about the count. If counting makes you tense, drop the numbers. Just make each out-breath a little longer than the in-breath.

What if 6 seconds feels too long? Try 3-5 first

If a 6-count exhale leaves you short of air, keep the shape and shorten the counts: in for 3, out for 5. That's 8 seconds per breath, or about 7 to 8 breaths a minute, which is still slow breathing.

A gentle progression looks like this:

  1. 3-5 until it feels easy
  2. 4-6, the standard pattern
  3. 5-7 (about 5 breaths a minute), optional, only if 4-6 starts to feel effortless

You can set any of these on the custom breathing timer. Comfort beats hitting exact numbers. A relaxed 3-5 does more for you than a strained 4-6.

4-6 breathing vs 4-7-8 vs box breathing: which is gentler?

For most people, 4-6 breathing is the gentlest of the common patterns because it has no breath holds. There's nothing to hold through and only two numbers to track, which suits people who find holds uncomfortable or who get a little panicky when air is "stopped."

Why does a longer exhale help? A short, honest summary

The honest answer is that the slow pace seems to do most of the work. A longer exhale may add to how relaxed you feel, but it hasn't shown a clear extra benefit over equal in and out breaths.

The case for slow breathing is fairly solid. A review of 15 studies found that breathing at under 10 breaths a minute was associated with higher heart rate variability (HRV) and more comfort and relaxation, though the evidence base was small. A larger meta-analysis of 223 studies found that vagally mediated HRV rose during slow breathing, right after a single session, and after multi-session practice.

The exhale ratio is more mixed:

  • In a study of 30 people, a long exhale produced more self-reported relaxation and less stress than a long inhale, with an HRV gain that appeared only at 6 breaths a minute.
  • In a randomised trial with 94 adults analysed, anxiety fell in both the longer-exhale and equal-breathing groups, with no significant difference between them.
  • A comparison of 1:1 and 1:2 breathing at 6 breaths a minute (26 people, plus a replication with 16) found no difference in HRV.
  • One study even found an HRV advantage for equal breathing at 5.5 breaths a minute.

The usual explanation involves the vagus nerve and the rest-and-digest side of the nervous system. It's plausible and partly supported, but not settled. Practically: slow down first, and use the longer exhale because it feels good, not because it's magic.

How long should you do 4-6 breathing?

One minute, or six breaths, is enough for a mid-scroll reset. It's short enough to actually do and long enough to notice a shift.

If you want a longer practice, the NHS suggests repeating calming breathing for at least 5 minutes and notes it can be done anywhere, and the PPT guide suggests building up gradually to 10 minutes.

Keep expectations proportional. A meta-analysis of breathwork trials found a small-to-medium reduction in stress (12 RCTs, 785 adults), with most studies at moderate risk of bias. That's encouraging, and still developing.

A quick safety note

4-6 breathing is gentle, but stop and go back to normal breathing if you feel dizzy, lightheaded, tingly or short of air, or have any chest discomfort. Sit or lie down while you're learning. If you're pregnant, have a heart or lung condition, or have a history of fainting or panic attacks, check with a clinician before making any breathing practice a regular habit.

Breathing can help you feel calmer and may reduce stress, but it doesn't treat anxiety, panic or insomnia. If anxiety, distress or sleep problems keep coming back, talk to a professional. If you're in crisis, call 988 in the US or your local emergency services.

Frequently asked questions

Is 4-6 breathing the same as 4-7-8?

No. 4-6 breathing is in for 4 and out for 6 with no holds, at about 6 breaths a minute. 4-7-8 is in for 4, hold for 7, out for 8, which works out to about 3 breaths a minute. The long hold makes 4-7-8 harder for many people and more likely to cause lightheadedness, so 4-6 is usually the easier one to start with or to use mid-scroll.

Is 4-6 breathing the same as coherent or resonance breathing?

They're close relatives. Coherent or resonance breathing is usually done at about 6 breaths a minute with equal in and out breaths, such as 5-5. 4-6 breathing has the same pace but a longer exhale. Studies comparing equal and longer-exhale breathing at this pace have found no clear heart rate variability advantage for the longer exhale, and one small study favoured equal breathing, so pick whichever feels more comfortable.

Why do I feel dizzy or short of air doing 4-6 breathing?

Usually the breaths are too big or the counts are too long for you right now. Gulping a full inhale or pushing out a long exhale can tip you into over-breathing or air hunger. Stop and breathe normally, then try again with softer breaths at about 70-80% of a full breath, or shorten the counts to 3-5. If dizziness keeps happening, check with a doctor.

Can 4-6 breathing help me fall asleep?

It may help you feel calmer as you wind down, which some people find makes it easier to drift off. Try a few minutes lying down with soft breaths and no pressure to count perfectly. It isn't a treatment for insomnia, though. If you regularly struggle to fall or stay asleep, talk to a doctor or sleep professional.

What's the difference between 4-6 and 4-4-6 or 4-2-6 breathing?

All three use a longer out-breath, but 4-4-6 adds a 4-count hold after the inhale and 4-2-6 adds a 2-count hold. The holds slow the pace further and add a step to track. 4-6 has no holds at all, so it's the simplest version and the one to choose if holding your breath feels uncomfortable.
