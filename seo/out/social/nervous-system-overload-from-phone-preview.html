That wired-but-flat feeling after "one quick check" has a plain name: nervous system overload. Not a diagnosis, just a name.

It tends to show up in the body first. Back teeth pressed together, shoulders creeping up, a buzzy head that won't settle on one thing.

The useful part: the moment you put the phone down seems to be a pressure point of its own. So that's where a short reset earns its keep, not at a gate before you open the app, when you're usually fine anyway.

On the evidence: a meta-analysis of 223 studies found slow breathing raises vagally mediated heart rate variability. Whether a longer exhale adds extra benefit is still early and mixed. We use 4 in, 6 out because it's comfortable, not because it's proven better.

Two common mistakes: forcing the exhale (six counts should feel like a slow leak, not a push) and glancing at the feed between breaths. Turn the phone face down for the minute.

If anxiety, sleep problems or distress keep coming back, talk to a professional. A breathing reset is for everyday overstimulation, not a replacement for care.

Ever checked your phone for one thing and looked up 20 minutes later with a tight jaw and a buzzy head? You don't need to delete anything to come back down.

Early research suggests scrolling can keep your body quietly switched on, and the moment you stop can bring a small spike of stress. That makes mid-scroll a good place for a reset.

Try it: drop your shoulders, part your back teeth, look up and widen your gaze, then take six slow breaths, 4 counts in and 6 out. About a minute. Then decide on purpose whether to keep scrolling.
