You meant to check one thing. Twenty minutes later your jaw is tight and your inner critic is already writing the review.

Two things are happening at once. The feed has wound you up, and now you're judging yourself for being wound up. The first is a body state. The second is a story about what that state says about you.

For what it's worth, large studies find that links between technology use and wellbeing are small at most. They're correlational, though, and a specific feed can still leave you feeling worse. So take the tense feeling seriously and go easy on the verdict.

Weekly limits tend to miss the moment. They arrive days after the comedown, when all that's left is the grade. In a study of friction apps, a message asking people to reflect didn't work on its own. A short pause with an easy way out worked best.

That's the idea behind DeRot, coming soon to iOS: it pauses your apps while you reset with a few calming breaths, so the wired feeling can fade before you decide what's next.

Keep the breaths easy, not big gulps. If you feel dizzy or lightheaded, stop and breathe normally.

That stab of guilt when your weekly screen time report comes in high? It's usually about a number. What you actually feel after a long scroll is the comedown: a tight jaw, raised shoulders, shallow breathing.

Guilt doesn't ease that. In research on parents, guilt about screen time predicted stress a month later, while the hours themselves had little to no link to either.

Try a gentler question: how do I feel when I put it down? When you notice the wired feeling mid-scroll, breathe in for 4 and out for 6, three to five times (about 30 to 50 seconds). Then decide whether to keep going. Either answer is fine.
